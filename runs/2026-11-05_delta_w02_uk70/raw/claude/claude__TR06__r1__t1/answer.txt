Several online jewelers disclose treatments clearly. Here are the ones my search turned up, plus how to check any seller yourself.


Sellers with clear disclosure

Angara lists an “Enhancement” field for each gemstone on its product certificate pages. One sapphire pendant, for example, shows “Heated” for every sapphire in it. Its listing for a GIA-certified sapphire ring shows the same field, so it appears to be standard practice.
Allurez publishes a treatment guide. It says sapphire, amethyst, aquamarine and citrine are heat-treated, with sapphire, zircon and black diamond treated unless the product details say they are unheated.
TopGem (UK) discloses and sets limits. It sells heated rubies and sapphires but not beryllium-heated stones, lead-glass-filled stones, irradiated, dyed or composite stones.
NNJ Designs puts a “Treatment” field in its loose sapphire listings. It states “Heated” with a note that the treatment is permanent and stable.
Anueva Jewelry states treatment in its listings. Its Montana sapphire listing says the stone has been heated, and others are marked untreated.
I didn’t find enough on larger retailers like Blue Nile, James Allen or Brilliant Earth to say anything reliable, so check their listings directly.


What the law requires

In the US, FTC guidelines require disclosure of any enhancement that dramatically affects a gemstone’s value, and whether the treatment is permanent. Heating is treated differently. Because it is so pervasive in ruby, sapphire and tanzanite, the Guides have historically treated it as an industry norm that doesn’t require routine disclosure. A seller can therefore stay silent on heating and still comply. Glass-filled rubies and diffusion treatments carry more value risk, so disclosure matters more there.


How to vet any seller

Look for plain wording such as “heated,” “no indications of heating,” “filled,” “oiled” or “treatment unknown.” If a listing says nothing at all, ask before buying.
For e-commerce, disclosures should appear before checkout, ideally in the product title, such as “fracture-filled.”
For expensive stones, ask for an independent lab report (GIA, AGL, SSEF and similar). Seller-written descriptions are weaker evidence than a report.
Be extra careful with ruby (glass filling) and emerald (oiling). Filled stones can need different care and are worth less than comparable unfilled ones.
