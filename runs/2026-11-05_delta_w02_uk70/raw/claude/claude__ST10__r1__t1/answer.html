Rubies are a great choice for an engagement ring. At 9 on the Mohs scale they're second only to diamond in hardness, so they hold up to daily wear. What matters most is the seller, because the ruby market has more treatment and disclosure pitfalls than the diamond market.

Good places to buy

  • Specialist colored-gemstone retailers such as Angara, Brilliant Earth, Ritani, or Blue Nile. They sell rubies in standard settings, usually with clear treatment disclosures and solid return policies, and they're a low-risk starting point.
  • Independent jewelers who specialize in colored stones. A good one can show you several stones in person, which matters because ruby color varies a lot. Look for membership in a trade body and a willingness to answer detailed questions.
  • Custom or bespoke jewelers. If you want a particular stone and design, you can choose the ruby first and then have a setting made around it.
  • Estate and antique dealers or reputable auction houses. These can offer beautiful, unusual pieces, but they suit buyers who are comfortable evaluating stones or who have an independent appraisal done.

What to check, wherever you buy

  • A lab report. For anything of real value, ask for a report from a respected gem lab such as GIA, SSEF, Gübelin, or AGL. It should state whether the stone is natural or synthetic, its origin, and any treatments.
  • Treatment disclosure. Most rubies are heat-treated, which is normal and accepted. Avoid lead-glass-filled rubies, which are much less durable and are sometimes sold cheaply without clear labeling. If the price seems too good for the size, this is often why.
  • Color over size. A vivid, saturated red with good clarity is worth far more than a larger, dull or brownish stone. Look at it in person or on video in different lighting.
  • Return policy and insurance. Make sure you can return or exchange it, and get it appraised and insured afterward.

Lab-created rubies are also worth considering. They're chemically identical to natural rubies, much cheaper, and free of the origin concerns, though they won't hold value the way a fine natural stone can.
