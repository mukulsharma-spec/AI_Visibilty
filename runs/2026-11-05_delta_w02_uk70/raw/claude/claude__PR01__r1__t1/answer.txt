There isn’t one best ring, because it depends on what you want, and my search didn’t turn up a specific ring I could verify as a standout under £1,500. What I can give you is how the budget tends to break down and where to look.

What £1,500 buys

Natural blue sapphire (usually heat-treated) in 9ct or 18ct gold: At this budget you’re typically looking at a smaller stone, around 0.5 to 1 carat, often with small diamond accents. Sapphires rank 9 on the Mohs scale, making them nearly as durable as diamonds, so they suit everyday wear. [clevelandheart]
Lab-grown sapphire: This is where you get the most size and clarity for the money. Lab stones keep the same composition as mined ones, and a larger stone is realistic here. Check for a certificate, such as GRC, which some sellers include.
Antique or vintage: Often the best value if you like older styles. A 19th-century sapphire and diamond three-stone ring at Fellows (a Birmingham auction house) had an estimate of £400 to £600. Ask about condition and repairs, and how to insure it. [fellows]

Where to look in the UK

Specialists like Mozeris (viewings by appointment in London and Essex) are worth visiting for natural or antique stones. They note that many buyers start from around £1,000 for a quality sapphire and diamond ring. [mozerisfineantiques]
Holts Gems sells sapphire and diamond trilogies and offers free resizing and cleaning.
Auction houses such as Fellows or Noonans can be the cheapest route to a genuine antique, but you’re buying as-seen, so view in person if you can.

What to prioritise

Colour: A rich, even medium-to-vivid blue matters more than carat weight. Avoid stones that look very dark or washed out.
Cut: A well-cut smaller stone looks livelier than a poorly cut larger one.
Treatment disclosure: Heat treatment is standard and fine, but the seller should state it clearly.
Certification: For anything natural over about £1,000, ask for a lab report or at least a written description.
