It can be, but a £2,000 purchase from an unknown site deserves real homework first. Plenty of small jewellers are legitimate, and plenty of scam sites look polished, so the look of the site tells you very little.

Before you buy, check:

Company registration. Search the business name on Companies House (UK). A real UK seller should have a registered company and a physical address you can verify on Google Maps. Be wary of a PO box or no address at all.

Domain age. Run the site through a WHOIS lookup. A domain registered a few months ago selling high-value jewellery is a red flag.

Independent reviews. Look at Trustpilot, Google reviews and Reddit, but search “[site name] scam” and “[site name] reviews” too. Be sceptical of sites with only glowing reviews that all sound alike.

Certification. For a gemstone at this price, a grading report from a recognised lab (GIA, IGI, SSEF, Gübelin, or the Gem-A/GGTL in the UK) should be offered, with a report number you can verify on the lab’s own website. A “certificate” from an unknown lab is worthless.

Policies. Look for a clear returns policy, ideally 14 days or more, with insurance on shipping, plus a phone number that someone actually answers.

Prices that seem too good. If similar stones elsewhere cost noticeably more, ask why.

Protect yourself when paying:

Use a credit card. Under Section 75 of the Consumer Credit Act, the card company is jointly liable for purchases between £100 and £30,000, which gives you strong recourse if something goes wrong. A debit card gives you chargeback rights, which are decent but less robust. PayPal Goods & Services also offers protection.

Never pay by bank transfer, crypto or gift cards. These are the standard routes for scams because they’re essentially irreversible.

Ask about shipping. It should be tracked, insured and signature-required.

Red flags that mean walk away: pressure to buy quickly, pushing you toward bank transfer, no verifiable address, no named people behind the business, or vague answers to specific questions about the stone.

Even if the seller is legitimate, you still want to confirm the stone is what’s described. Consider having an independent valuation done once it arrives, and check you can return it if the valuation comes in short.
