Several online sellers offer colored gemstones with independent lab reports, but it usually varies stone by stone, so check each listing.

  • Leibish & Co. specializes in fancy colored diamonds and colored gemstones. Many of its colored stones are GIA certified and untreated. learningjewelry

  • Africagems says buyers should require full treatment disclosure and independent certification from respected labs like GIA or AGL. africagems
  • Diamondspot (Europe) includes a report from its in-house lab with every piece, sometimes supplemented by GIA, GRS, AGL, or other international certificates. For colored gemstones, that in-house report is not independent. diamondspot

Which labs matter for colored stones

  • GIA is the best-known lab. Note that it announced new colored stone reports and expanded origin services effective January 2026. Existing GIA reports remain valid. indianjewellerskyjems
  • GRS is particularly renowned for certifying rubies, sapphires, and emeralds, and its reports cover origin, quality, and treatments. jupitergem
  • AGL was established in 1977 in New York City and specializes in colored gemstones. starruby
  • Gübelin and SSEF are also well respected for high-value stones, though I didn’t find them in these results.

Buying tips

  • Look for a report from an independent lab (GIA, GRS, AGL, Gübelin, SSEF). An in-house report from the seller is much weaker.
  • Check that the report number matches the stone, and verify it on the lab’s website.
  • For rubies, sapphires, and emeralds, origin and treatment (heated, unheated, oil or resin filling) can change the price a lot. Reports are most worthwhile for stones of significant value.
  • Smaller or lower-priced stones often come without a lab report, because the cost of testing is high relative to the stone’s value.
