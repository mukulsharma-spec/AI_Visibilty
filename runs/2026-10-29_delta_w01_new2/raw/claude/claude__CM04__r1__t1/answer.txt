Between those two, I’d lean Blue Nile, but for rubies specifically there are better options.

Kay vs. Blue Nile

Both are now owned by Signet, which finalized its Blue Nile purchase in 2025 and plans to position Blue Nile as the higher-end brand. Kay is a mall chain with in-store try-on and financing, but it tends to carry smaller, lower-grade rubies, often lab-created or heavily treated, in fashion-style settings with high markups. Blue Nile has better build-your-own options and clearer online listings, but its colored gemstone selection is much thinner than its diamond selection.

What matters most with a ruby

- Treatment. Most rubies are heat-treated, which is normal and fine if disclosed. Avoid “lead-glass filled” or “fracture-filled” stones, which are cheap and fragile.
- Natural vs. lab-grown. Lab rubies are chemically identical and much cheaper, so you get a bigger, cleaner stone for the money. Natural rubies, especially unheated or Burmese ones, are far pricier and hold value better.
- Color. A rich, slightly purplish red usually beats a pale pinkish one. Good color matters more than size.
- Certification. For anything over a few hundred dollars, ask for a report from GIA, AGL, or a similar lab, since many retailers just give their own appraisal.

Places I’d look instead

- Angara: specializes in colored gemstones, grades them by quality tier, and has a big ruby selection with customizable settings. It’s probably the best all-around choice.
- Brilliant Earth: good for both natural and lab-grown rubies, with solid return policies.
- Ross-Simons: affordable, treated natural rubies, good for a budget.
- Etsy or Ruby Lane: independent sellers and vintage pieces, where you can often find better value or character. Check seller reviews and ask for lab reports.
- A local independent jeweler: useful if you want to see the stone in person or have something custom made.
