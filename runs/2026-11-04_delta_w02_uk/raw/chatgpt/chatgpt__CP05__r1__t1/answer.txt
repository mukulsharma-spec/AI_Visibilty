Is tanzanite a good stone for an engagement ring? I'm shopping in the UK.
Yes! Tanzanite can make a beautiful and distinctive engagement ring, particularly if you love rich violet-blue gemstones and want something a little different from a traditional diamond or sapphire.
However, there is one important consideration: tanzanite is considerably less durable than diamond or sapphire, so I would be a little cautious about choosing it for a ring you intend to wear every single day for decades.
Here's what I'd consider before buying one in the UK.
1. Is tanzanite durable enough for an engagement ring?
12
Tanzanite has a Mohs hardness of 6–7, compared with:
Diamond: 10
Sapphire: 9
Ruby: 9
Emerald: 7.5–8
Tanzanite: 6–7
Hardness isn't the only measure of durability. Tanzanite also has cleavage, meaning it can split or chip if struck at the wrong angle.
This matters because engagement rings are exposed to everyday knocks, accidental bumps and contact with hard surfaces.

2. What makes tanzanite special?
10
Tanzanite is especially beautiful because of its colour-changing appearance.
Depending on the lighting and viewing angle, it can show:
Deep royal blue.
Violet and purple.
Blue with subtle burgundy or reddish flashes.
The finest stones tend to have a rich, saturated blue-violet colour rather than looking pale or washed out.
It's also relatively rare geographically: commercially significant tanzanite comes from a small mining area near Mount Kilimanjaro in Tanzania.
3. If you choose tanzanite, how should you set it?
This is particularly important for protecting the stone.
12
My recommendations:
Bezel setting: My first choice. A metal rim protects the edges of the gemstone.
Halo setting: Small surrounding diamonds can offer some protection, although the centre stone still needs protection.
Low-profile setting: Avoid a tall, exposed stone that can catch on clothing or get knocked.
Protective claws: If you prefer a solitaire, choose a setting with sturdy, well-positioned prongs.
For the metal, platinum or 18ct gold would both be lovely.
I'd avoid delicate high-set solitaire designs for a stone as vulnerable as tanzanite.
4. What about buying tanzanite in the UK?
I'd look for a reputable jeweller who can provide:
Independent gemstone identification or a reliable gemmological report.
Disclosure of whether the stone has been heat-treated (a common and accepted treatment for tanzanite).
Clear information about any treatments or enhancements.
A written warranty and repair policy.
A sensible return period.
An appraisal or valuation for insurance.
One particularly important question: does the jeweller's insurance or warranty cover accidental damage to the gemstone?
Don't assume it does.
