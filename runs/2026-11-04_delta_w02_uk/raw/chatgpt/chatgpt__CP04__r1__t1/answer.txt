What should I look for when buying a sapphire online? I'm shopping in the UK.
Buying a sapphire online in the UK can be a great way to find a beautiful stone, often with more choice than you'd get in a high-street jeweller. But there are a few things to watch out for: colour, treatments, origin, certification and the seller's return policy.
My biggest advice is: don't buy a sapphire based on carat weight or photographs alone. Two sapphires of the same weight can look dramatically different in person and have very different values.
Here's what I'd look for.
1. Colour: the most important factor
10
Colour is usually the biggest influence on a sapphire's beauty and desirability.
Look for:
Royal blue: Rich, deep blue, often highly sought after.
Cornflower blue: Softer, vivid blue, sometimes with a slightly violet tone.
Velvety blue: A rich, slightly silky appearance.
Teal: Blue-green tones, increasingly popular.
Pastel blue: Lighter, delicate and elegant.
What to avoid:
Very dark stones that look almost black.
Stones that appear grey or lifeless.
Colour that looks dramatically different between photographs.
Excessively saturated, unnatural-looking colours without a clear explanation.
My tip: Ask for a video of the sapphire in natural daylight, ideally next to a neutral white background. This helps you assess its true colour.
2. Natural versus lab-grown
This is an important distinction.
Natural sapphire
Mined from the earth. Usually more expensive, particularly when untreated and of fine quality.
Lab-grown sapphire
Created in a laboratory. Chemically and physically sapphire, but generally much less expensive.
Neither is inherently better. It depends on what matters to you.
Choose natural if you value rarity, geological origin and potential collectability.
Choose lab-grown if you want a beautiful sapphire at a substantially lower price.
Make sure the listing explicitly states which it is. Terms such as "created sapphire" or "synthetic sapphire" generally mean lab-grown.

An untreated natural sapphire with attractive colour and clarity can command a significant premium.
Heat-treated sapphires are also perfectly legitimate purchases, provided the treatment is disclosed and the price reflects it.
I'd be much more cautious about diffusion-treated or fracture-filled stones, particularly if you're paying natural, untreated sapphire prices.
4. Certification: don't skip this
7
For an expensive sapphire, I would strongly recommend an independent gemmological laboratory report.
Look for reports from reputable laboratories such as:
GIA
SSEF
Gübelin Gem Lab
AGL
A good report should clarify:
Whether the stone is natural or synthetic.
Whether treatments have been detected.
Whether the stone's identity is consistent with sapphire.
Origin, if an origin opinion has been provided.
For expensive stones, don't rely solely on a seller's own certificate.
Important: A certificate isn't a guarantee of value. It establishes gemmological information, not whether the asking price is fair.
5. Clarity: some inclusions are normal
12
Unlike diamonds, sapphires don't have a universally used consumer clarity grading scale.
Look for:
Good transparency rather than a cloudy or milky appearance.
No obvious cracks reaching the surface.
No distracting dark inclusions in the centre.
An attractive appearance when viewed face-up.
A few tiny natural inclusions are perfectly normal. Don't pay a huge premium for a supposedly flawless stone without independent verification.
6. Cut and dimensions
The cut affects how attractive and lively the sapphire looks.
8
Popular shapes include:
Oval – classic and elegant.
Cushion – softer, vintage appearance.
Round – traditional and symmetrical.
Emerald/octagonal – sophisticated and geometric.
Pear – distinctive and feminine.
Ask for dimensions in millimetres as well as carat weight. A deep-cut sapphire can weigh more without looking particularly large.
7. Origin: does it matter?
You may see sellers advertising sapphires from:
Sri Lanka (Ceylon) – known for a wide range of blue sapphires.
Kashmir – historically famous for exceptional velvety blue sapphires.
Madagascar – an important source of modern sapphire production.
Myanmar – another historic sapphire-producing region.
Australia – often associated with darker blue sapphires.
Origin can influence price, but it doesn't automatically determine quality.
Be especially cautious about claims of Kashmir origin. Genuine Kashmir sapphires are exceptionally rare and valuable. An independent laboratory origin opinion is important if you're paying a premium for it.
8. UK-specific things to check
This is where I'd be particularly careful when buying online.
Returns and consumer protection
When buying from a UK business online, you generally have 14 days from receiving the item to notify the seller that you wish to cancel, followed by another 14 days to return it. Bespoke and personalised items can be exceptions.
