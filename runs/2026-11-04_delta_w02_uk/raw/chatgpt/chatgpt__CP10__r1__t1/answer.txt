The biggest thing that determines the price of a sapphire is how rare, beautiful and natural the stone is. Two sapphires can look almost identical in a jewellery shop but have very different values because of their origin, treatment, colour and quality.
If you're shopping in the UK, there are a few things worth understanding before spending money, particularly because sapphire pricing can vary enormously.
1. Colour – usually the biggest factor
For blue sapphires, colour is generally the most important consideration.
Cornflower blue: A beautiful, velvety medium blue, traditionally highly prized.
Royal blue: Rich, vivid and saturated blue. Often commands premium prices.
Kashmir blue: Famous for its velvety, slightly hazy blue appearance. Exceptionally rare and expensive.
Dark blue: Can be attractive, but very dark stones tend to be less valuable.
Pale blue: Often less expensive, although exceptional stones can still command high prices.
The sweet spot is generally a vivid, saturated blue that isn't so dark that it loses its brilliance.

Unheated: The sapphire has not undergone heat treatment to improve its colour or clarity. Fine examples are particularly valuable.
Heated: Heat treatment is a widely accepted industry practice that improves colour and/or clarity. These are still genuine natural sapphires.
Diffusion-treated: Colour has been artificially enhanced using elements that penetrate the gemstone. These are generally worth considerably less.
Lab-grown: Chemically and physically sapphire, but created in a laboratory rather than mined. Much cheaper.
Be careful with descriptions such as "natural sapphire" – this doesn't automatically mean unheated.
3. Origin – where it comes from
Some origins carry a significant premium:
Kashmir: Extremely rare, historically prestigious and often exceptionally expensive.
Sri Lanka (Ceylon): Highly regarded for beautiful, bright blue sapphires.
Myanmar (Burma): Known for some exceptional blue sapphires.
Madagascar: Produces some excellent sapphires, often offering good value.
Australia: Often produces darker blue sapphires.
Importantly, origin alone doesn't guarantee quality. An exceptional Madagascan sapphire can be more desirable than a mediocre Sri Lankan one.
4. Clarity – how clean the stone looks
Sapphires naturally contain internal characteristics called inclusions.
Eye-clean stones (no obvious inclusions to the naked eye) are generally more valuable.
Tiny inclusions visible only under magnification are usually acceptable.
Large cracks, cloudy areas and obvious imperfections reduce value.
One important point: don't assume a completely flawless sapphire is necessarily natural. Lab-grown sapphires can look exceptionally clean.
5. Cut – how well the sapphire has been shaped
A good cut makes a sapphire sparkle and distributes its colour evenly.
Look for:
Even colour across the stone.
Good brightness rather than a dark, lifeless centre.
No obvious transparent window in the middle.
Symmetrical facets and a pleasing shape.
A poorly cut 2-carat sapphire can be worth less than a beautifully cut 1-carat sapphire.
6. Carat weight – bigger isn't always better
Larger fine-quality sapphires are rarer, so their price per carat generally increases with size.
For example, a 3-carat sapphire with exceptional colour can be disproportionately more expensive than a 1-carat stone of similar quality.
But I'd prioritise colour and cut over size. A smaller, beautiful sapphire often looks more impressive.
7. Certification – particularly important in the UK
For an expensive purchase, I would want an independent gemmological report.
Some reputable laboratories include:
GIA.
SSEF.
Gübelin Gem Lab.
GRS.
Ideally, the report should establish:
Whether the sapphire is natural or laboratory-grown.
Whether it has been heat-treated.
Whether other treatments have been detected.
Its weight and measurements.
Geographic origin, if the laboratory can determine it.
A certificate isn't a guarantee that the asking price is fair. It verifies important characteristics that help you assess the price.

My biggest piece of advice: Don't automatically pay a huge premium for an unheated sapphire. A beautiful, well-cut, heat-treated Sri Lankan sapphire can be a wonderful purchase. You don't need to pay collector prices to get a stunning gemstone.
