Sapphire pricing comes down to a handful of factors, and they don’t all carry equal weight.

Color matters most. The most valuable sapphires have a vivid, saturated, medium-to-medium-dark blue, often described as “cornflower” (slightly violet-leaning blue, associated with Kashmir and some Burmese stones) or “royal blue.” Stones that are too pale, too dark (inky), or tinged with green or grey drop sharply in price. Other colors (“fancy” sapphires like pink, yellow, or green) are priced on their own scale, and the rare padparadscha (a pinkish-orange) can outprice blue.

Clarity comes next, but sapphires are expected to have some inclusions. Eye-clean stones command a premium, while visible inclusions or a cloudy, “sleepy” look reduce value. Fine silk (tiny needle-like inclusions) can be a good sign in an unheated stone, but excessive silk makes a stone look milky.

Treatment is a big one. Most sapphires on the market are heat-treated to improve color and clarity, which is accepted and widely disclosed. Unheated stones are much rarer and can cost significantly more, often by a wide margin at higher quality. Beware of beryllium diffusion or fracture-filling, which should be heavily discounted and must be disclosed.

Origin can add a premium, particularly Kashmir, Burma (Myanmar), and unheated Ceylon (Sri Lanka). Auction houses and collectors pay a lot for these, but it only really matters with a reputable lab report backing the claim. For most buyers, a beautiful Sri Lankan, Madagascan, or Thai stone is better value than chasing a famous origin.

Carat weight affects price per carat, which rises as stones get bigger because large, clean, fine-color sapphires are rare. Cut quality also matters: a well-proportioned stone that shows even color and good brilliance is worth more than a deep or shallow cut with a “window” or dark zones.

Synthetic vs. natural is the most basic divide. Lab-created sapphires are chemically identical but cost a small fraction of natural ones, so make sure you know which you’re getting.

Shopping in the UK, a few practical tips:

Ask for a report from a recognized lab (Gübelin, SSEF, GIA, or the Gem & Jewellery Institute / GemmoLogical labs) for any significant purchase. Reports should state heat treatment status and origin if it matters to you. Buy from jewellers who are members of trade bodies like the National Association of Jewellers (NAJ) or the Gemmological Association of Great Britain (Gem-A) fellows. Hatton Garden in London is a long-established hub with plenty of competition, so it’s worth comparing a few dealers. Get the stone’s full details (weight, dimensions, treatment, origin) in writing on the invoice, and check the return policy.
