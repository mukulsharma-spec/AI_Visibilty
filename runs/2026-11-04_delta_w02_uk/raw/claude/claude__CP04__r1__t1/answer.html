Here’s what matters most, roughly in order of importance.

1. Treatments and disclosure
Nearly all sapphires on the market are heat-treated, and that’s normal and widely accepted as long as it’s disclosed. What you want to avoid are less stable or lower-value treatments, such as lead-glass filling, fracture filling, or beryllium diffusion, which should be priced far lower and are often not disclosed by dodgy sellers. Unheated stones command a big premium, so only pay it with a lab report confirming “no indications of heating.”

2. Certification
For anything over a few hundred pounds, ask for a report from a reputable lab such as GIA, SSEF, Gübelin, AGL, GRS, or Lotus. In the UK, Gem-A and some assay offices also offer testing. A report should confirm the stone is natural (not synthetic), state the treatment, and ideally the origin. Be wary of in-house “certificates” from the seller or lesser-known labs, which are often generous with their grading.

3. Colour
Colour drives most of the value. Look at hue, tone (how light or dark), and saturation (how vivid). The best blues are vivid and medium in tone. Stones that are too dark look inky in low light, and too pale ones look washed out. Sapphires also come in pink, yellow, padparadscha (pink-orange), and other colours, which are all priced differently.

4. Cut and proportions
A well-cut stone is lively across its whole face. Watch for “windowing” (a see-through patch in the centre), dark extinction areas, and overly deep stones that carry hidden weight, which means you pay for carats you can’t see. Ask for dimensions in millimetres, not just carat weight.

5. Clarity
Sapphires usually have some inclusions, which is normal. What matters is whether it’s “eye-clean” at normal viewing distance. Fine “silk” can actually add a soft glow, but cracks or large visible inclusions affect durability and price.

6. Origin
Origin like Sri Lanka (Ceylon), Burma, Kashmir, or Madagascar can add significant value, especially Kashmir and Burma. It only counts if a lab report states it, so don’t pay an origin premium on the seller’s word alone.

7. Buying online specifically

Ask for a video in natural light and artificial light, since photos can be heavily edited or lit to flatter.
Check the returns policy. In the UK, the Consumer Contracts Regulations give you a 14-day cancellation right on distance purchases, though custom-made items are excluded. Many good sellers offer longer.
Check the seller’s reputation: reviews, membership of trade bodies like Gem-A or the National Association of Jewellers, and how long they’ve traded.
If buying from overseas (Sri Lanka and Thailand are common sources), factor in import VAT, courier fees, and insurance. Make sure shipping is tracked and insured.
If buying set jewellery, precious metal items over the weight thresholds must be hallmarked in the UK.
Beware of “too good to be true” prices, which usually mean glass-filled, synthetic, or misrepresented stones.
