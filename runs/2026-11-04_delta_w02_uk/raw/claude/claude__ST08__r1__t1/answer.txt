Natural alexandrite is rare and expensive, so where you buy and what paperwork you get matters a lot. Here are the main routes in the UK.

Where to look

Specialist and antique jewellers, such as Hancocks (Burlington Arcade) and Bentley & Skinner (Piccadilly), regularly handle coloured gemstones and estate pieces. Both can source on request.
Hatton Garden, London, has many independent gem dealers and jewellers who can source or set a stone for you. Choose established dealers, and ask for a lab report.
Auction houses like Bonhams, Chiswick Auctions and Fellows (Birmingham) often have vintage and antique alexandrite rings. They are a good source for older natural stones, but check the lot notes and condition report.
Specialist online gem dealers are worth considering. Look for ones that publish lab reports and have a clear returns policy.

How to make sure it’s natural

Ask for an independent gemmological report from a lab such as Gem-A (London), GIA, SSEF, GRS or AGL. It should say “natural alexandrite” (chrysoberyl). For stones over about 1 carat, I’d treat a report as essential.
Watch for “alexandrite” that’s actually synthetic corundum. Cheap colour-change stones that shift from bluish-purple to pinkish-purple are usually synthetic corundum, not alexandrite at all.
Be suspicious of low prices. Natural alexandrite with a strong colour change (green or bluish-green in daylight, purplish-red in incandescent light) costs thousands of pounds per carat. A “bargain” is almost certainly synthetic.
Check the colour change yourself under daylight and warm incandescent light. The stronger and cleaner the shift, the more valuable the stone.
Look at origin and treatment. Sri Lanka, Brazil, India and East Africa are common sources, and old Russian (Ural) stones are the most prized. Ask about any treatment.

Buying tips

Get the report details, carat weight, and “natural, untreated” (or the treatment) written on the invoice.
Confirm the return policy. Online purchases in the UK generally carry a 14-day cancellation right, but ask about returns specifically if the stone is later found to be synthetic.
If you’re unsure about a piece, you can pay Gem-A or a Birmingham Assay Office gem testing service to verify it before you commit.

Do you have a rough budget or a preferred style, such as vintage, modern solitaire or a cluster? That would help narrow down where to look.
