Here are the main things to check, roughly in order of how much they tell you.

1. Read the wording on the listing carefully

Terminology is where most of the sleight of hand happens. In the UK, the Trade Descriptions rules and the Jewellery industry guidance (and the Federal Trade Commission rules if you ever buy from the US) require clear disclosure of anything that isn’t a plain natural stone. Watch for:

“Natural” means mined and formed without human involvement in creating the stone. If the listing just says “sapphire” with no qualifier, ask.
“Lab-created,” “lab-grown,” “synthetic,” “cultured” are real gemstones chemically, but not natural. They should be clearly labelled, not buried in small print.
“Simulant” or “imitation” (e.g. cubic zirconia, glass, moissanite sold as diamond) aren’t the real mineral at all.
“Treated,” “enhanced,” “heated,” “fracture-filled,” “diffusion-treated” mean the stone is natural but altered. Heat treatment is common and accepted for sapphire, ruby and many others, but it should be disclosed, and it affects value.
Vague trade names like “Swiss blue topaz,” “mystic topaz,” “created opal” or “emerald-green quartz” are often treated or lab-made material.

2. Ask for independent lab certification, and verify it

For any significant purchase, an independent report matters far more than the seller’s own claims. Reputable UK and international labs include:

Gem-A (the Gemmological Association, based in London)
SSEF, GRS, Gübelin, AGL for high-value coloured stones
GIA, IGI, HRD for diamonds

Then check that the report is genuine: look up the report number on the lab’s own website, and make sure the stone’s weight and measurements match the listing. A certificate from a lab you can’t find, or one issued by the seller’s own “in-house lab,” isn’t independent.

3. Check hallmarking (for the metal)

This doesn’t prove the gem is natural, but it’s a good indicator of a trustworthy seller. In the UK, gold, silver, platinum and palladium items over certain weights must carry a hallmark from an assay office (London, Birmingham, Sheffield or Edinburgh). A seller who is sloppy about legal hallmarking may be sloppy elsewhere.

4. Look at the seller, not just the stone

Trade memberships such as the National Association of Jewellers (NAJ), the Gem-A community, or the British Jewellers’ Association
A clear returns policy: UK distance selling rules give you 14 days to cancel most online purchases, though bespoke items may be excluded
Full contact details, a real UK address and a company number you can check at Companies House
Detailed, honest descriptions: carat weight, dimensions, origin (if known), treatments, and clear photos in natural light
Independent reviews on Trustpilot or similar, read critically rather than just by star rating

5. Use price as a sanity check

If a “natural” stone is priced at a fraction of what comparable stones cost elsewhere, there’s usually a reason. Big natural emeralds, rubies and sapphires without treatment are genuinely expensive, and lab-grown versions typically sell at a steep discount.

6. After you buy

If you want certainty, you can get the stone tested by an independent gemmologist (Gem-A can point you to qualified ones, and many UK assay offices and valuers offer testing). Keep the receipt and the original listing, because if the item turns out to be misdescribed you’re entitled to a remedy under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, and paying by credit card adds extra protection under Section 75 for purchases over £100.
