Here are the main ways to get the Blue Nile look for less in the UK, roughly from cheapest to priciest.

Budget (under about £300): sterling silver

- Daisy London has a sapphire tennis bracelet in sterling silver for £249. Its stones are lab-grown white sapphires, though, so it won’t give you the blue. - Ross-Simons (US) sells sterling silver blue sapphire tennis bracelets, some in the $140 to $500 range. They’re priced in dollars, so check whether they ship to the UK and what duty and VAT would add. - Silver is the biggest saving, but it’s softer than gold and can tarnish. Check whether it’s plated, since plating wears off with daily wear.

Mid-range (about £1,500 to £4,000): solid gold, lab-grown or smaller stones

- Angara’s UK site has a lab-grown blue sapphire and lab diamond tennis bracelet in 14ct white gold at £3,719. It also has a round lab-grown blue sapphire version at £3,519, which lists a GIA certificate. Angara is the closest direct competitor to Blue Nile in style and quality. - Ernest Jones had a 9ct white gold sapphire and 1ct diamond tennis bracelet at £1,625, marked down from £3,250. High street jewellers like Ernest Jones, Goldsmiths and The Jewellers run frequent sales, so wait for one if you can.

Ways to bring the price down on any of these

- Choose 9ct gold instead of 14ct or 18ct. It’s a bit paler and less rich in colour, but much cheaper. - Choose lab-grown sapphires. They’re chemically identical to mined ones and noticeably cheaper. - Choose smaller stones, such as 3mm rather than 4mm. This lowers the carat weight and the price a lot. - Consider sapphire-only rather than sapphire with diamond accents. - Check for a UK hallmark on gold pieces, and look at the return policy. Angara lists free 60-day returns in the UK.

I haven’t compared these against current Blue Nile prices, so check a comparable Blue Nile bracelet (same metal, stone size and carat weight) to see what you’d actually save.
